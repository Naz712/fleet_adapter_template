The math

    Why one long track tips. At a platform edge a straight track keeps climbing until its centre of mass passes the edge nosing. At that moment it is balanced on a single point, so it pivots through the whole change of slope in a few centimetres of travel and its far end drops onto the platform. Going down it is the same pivot in reverse: a nose-dive onto the stairs.

    What the hinge does instead. Split into two units, the change of slope happens in two halves, each spread over most of a unit length:

    1. Catch. At the foot of a flight the front unit tilts up onto the nosings while the rear stays flat, then the rear follows.
    2. Fold. Nearing an edge, the hinge lifts slightly and folds the leading unit down onto the next surface before the middle reaches the edge, so both ends stay on something.
    3. Straighten. Once the leading unit is along the straight, the hinge unbends and the trailing unit comes over the edge and lines up.

    How this simulation works. It is a slow-speed (quasi-static) model: at every centimetre it finds how both units rest on the real ES1 step geometry, then checks support, clearance and tipping. Tracks ride on the line joining the step nosings and bridge the gaps between them, which holds because each unit spans at least two nosings. The hinge is driven: it settles each unit onto the stairs, folds the leading unit over an edge, lets the trailing unit straighten, and can only bend so fast per metre travelled. Traction, motor power and impact forces are not modelled. To hold on the slope the tracks need a friction coefficient of at least 0.62 on the treads.
