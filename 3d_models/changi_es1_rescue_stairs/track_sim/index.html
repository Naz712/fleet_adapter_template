    The math

      Sitting and lying at once. The seat is a reclining stretcher-chair. At about 45° the person is half lying and half sitting: back reclined, knees a little raised, lower legs out in front. A driven pivot under the seat keeps it level, so the person stays at the same angle from the ground to the top platform. Their head is uphill and feet downhill, the way casualties are carried on stairs: they ride backwards going up and face forward coming down.

      Why one long track tips. At a platform edge a straight track keeps climbing until its centre of mass passes the edge nosing. At that moment it balances on a single point, so it pivots through the whole change of slope in a few centimetres and its far end drops onto the platform. The seat drive cannot turn that fast, so the person is thrown forward or back as well.

      What the hinge does instead. Split into two units, the change of slope happens in two halves, each spread over most of a unit length:

      1. Catch. At the foot of a flight the front unit tilts up onto the nosings while the rear stays flat, then the rear follows.
      2. Fold. Nearing an edge, the hinge lifts slightly and folds the leading unit down onto the next surface. It holds that fold until the centre of mass is 5 cm past the edge, so the trailing unit keeps its end on the stairs.
      3. Straighten. Then the hinge unbends and the trailing unit comes over the edge and lines up.

      What carrying this load takes

      How this simulation works. It is a slow-speed (quasi-static) model. At every centimetre it finds how both units rest on the real ES1 step geometry, then checks support, the gap around the seat and person, and tipping. Tracks ride on the line joining the step nosings and bridge the gaps between them, which holds because each unit spans at least two nosings. The person is a 1.80 m adult built from standard body-segment proportions. Motor torques and forces are worked out for slow, steady driving; impacts and braking are not modelled.
